PROJECT IDEA

What problem are we solving?

Reconnecting a dispersed heritage with its lost context.

The physical collection was divided; so was the record of it.

PeopleManuscriptsPlacesInstitutionsEventsConcepts

This project is therefore not simply a cultural heritage website. It reconstructs the connections between the manuscripts, the people who encountered and moved them, the institutions that now keep them, and the digital objects that make them accessible — as explicit, documented relations rather than prose.

Dunhuang belonged to the wider historical network of the Silk Road, while the Mogao Caves and the Library Cave form the manuscripts' more immediate spatial context.

The goal is to turn cultural heritage evidence into machine-readable structured knowledge.

GUIDING QUESTIONS

What are we reconnecting?

Five questions define what the project asks.

This project asks how the Dunhuang manuscripts can be reconnected with the Silk Road as their wider historical network, their original place, the people who encountered and dispersed them, and the institutions that preserve them today.

One Silk Road context. One collection. Many places. Many people. Many institutions. One reconstructed network.

Original Context / R1

The site at Sachu (modern-day Dunhuang) was an important regional centre for manuscript production during this period and had also served as an official printing office during the 8th and 9th centuries when the area was under Tibetan rule and formed part of the Silk Road network.

Source basis for R1: Dunhuang → part_of_network → Silk Road.

Discovery / R5

The documents in the cave were discovered by the Daoist monk Wang Yuanlu, who was interested in restoring the Mogao Caves, on 25 June 1900.

Evidence for discovery-event and R5.

Dispersal / R6-R8

From 1907 onwards, Wang began to sell them to Western explorers, notably Aurel Stein and Paul Pelliot. Hundreds more of the manuscripts were sold by Wang to Ōtani Kōzui.

Evidence for separate Stein, Pelliot, and Otani purchase events.

Beijing Transfer / R11 & R13

Due to the efforts of the scholar and antiquarian Luo Zhenyu, most of the remaining Chinese manuscripts were taken to Beijing in 1910 and are now in the National Library of China.

Source basis for the 1910 transfer to Beijing, Luo Zhenyu's influence, and the National Library of China keeper assertion shown in this excerpt.

Digital access / R14-R15

All of the manuscript collections are being digitized by the International Dunhuang Project, and can be freely accessed online.

Source basis for R14 and R15. R16 is an Enriched project interpretation supported by the International Dunhuang Project's institutional description, not Direct evidence from the frozen Wikipedia revision.
